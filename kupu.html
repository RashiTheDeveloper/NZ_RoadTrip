Kupu

Must-know Māori words

Te reo Māori is an official language. You will see it on road signs, museums, and greetings. Vowels are pure: a as in “car”, e as in “pet”, i as in “machine”, o as in “or”, u as in “put”. A macron (ā, ē, ī, ō, ū) means a longer vowel. “Wh” is usually an “f” sound. “Ng” is as in “singer”.

Kia ora
kee-ah or-ah
Hello, thanks, cheers — the everyday greeting. Use it.
Haere mai
high-reh my
Welcome; come here. You will hear it at a pōwhiri (welcome).
Aotearoa
ah-oh-teh-ah-raw-ah
Land of the long white cloud — a name for New Zealand.
Tēnā koe / kōrua / koutou
teh-nah kweh / kaw-roo-ah / kaw-toh
Formal hello to one person / two people / three or more.
Ka kite anō
kah kee-teh ah-naw
See you again.
Whānau
fah-noh
Family, including extended family.
Whenua
feh-noo-ah
Land; also placenta. The bond is the point.
Tangata whenua
tah-ngah-tah feh-noo-ah
People of the land; the iwi with authority in a place.
Iwi / hapū
ee-wee / hah-poo
Tribe / sub-tribe.
Marae
mah-rye
The courtyard and buildings of a community’s ceremonial home. Wait to be called on; do not wander across the marae ātea uninvited.
Hongi
hong-ee
The greeting of pressing noses and sharing breath.
Mana
mah-nah
Prestige, authority, spiritual power. You do not take it; you recognise it.
Tapu / noa
tah-poo / naw-ah
Sacred or restricted / ordinary and unrestricted. Do not sit on tables or pillows; food and sacred spaces are kept apart.
Whakapapa
fah-kah-pah-pah
Genealogy; the layers that connect people to land and ancestors.
Aroha
ah-raw-hah
Love, compassion, empathy.
Kia kaha
kee-ah kah-hah
Be strong.
Kai
kye
Food; to eat.
Moana / maunga / awa
maw-ah-nah / mau-ngah / ah-wah
Sea / mountain / river. These words are on countless maps.
Waka
wah-kah
Canoe; also used for vehicle. Ancestral waka are founding stories of iwi.
Haka
hah-kah
A posture dance of challenge, welcome, or mourning — not only the All Blacks version.
Pounamu
paw-nah-moo
Greenstone / nephrite jade, especially of Te Wai Pounamu (the South Island). Often gifted, not grabbed as a souvenir from riverbeds.
Kaitiaki
kye-tee-ah-kee
Guardian; the duty of care for a place or species.

If you are welcomed onto a marae, follow the kaikaranga and kaikōrero, remove hats, and stay for the meal that lifts tapu. Asking quietly is always better than assuming.

Pronunciations are approximations for English speakers. Local iwi may use different vowel lengths and names for the same mountain or lake.
